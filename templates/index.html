Domain spoofing: swaps a lowercase L for the digit 1, plus "-secure" tacked on to look official.
Urgency + fear: a manufactured deadline is designed to short-circuit careful thinking.
Generic greeting: real institutions holding your account usually know your actual name.
Vague threat: no specifics — just enough alarm to make you click without verifying.
Artificial time pressure, restated so it's the thing you remember, not the details.
Mismatched destination: a ".ru" domain with the brand name tacked on.
Threat Intelligence

Phishing Examples

How attackers manipulate, and what it looks like at scale.

Pretexting

Fabricated scenario

Inventing a believable situation to justify asking for information they shouldn't need.

Baiting

Too good to ignore

A free gift card or shocking subject line — curiosity or greed does the work fear usually does.

Impersonation / BEC

Business Email Compromise

Posing as a CEO or vendor to request an urgent transfer, timed for when the real person is traveling.

Spear phishing

Targeted vs. mass

Personalized using real details about you or your company — far harder to catch.

BUSINESS EMAIL COMPROMISE · TECH SECTOR · 2013–2015

Between 2013 and 2015, a fraudster impersonated a hardware supplier that two major tech companies regularly paid, sending convincing fake invoices through correspondence that closely mimicked the real vendor. Finance staff processed the payments as routine transactions.

Over roughly two years, the scheme diverted more than $100 million before it was uncovered and prosecuted. Most funds were recovered and the perpetrator convicted, but the case remains one of the most cited examples of how patient impersonation — no malware, no hacking — can succeed purely through convincing correspondence and trust.

Paraphrased summary; search "Evaldas Rimasauskas" for full reporting.

Awareness

Security Checklist

What to actually do.

  • Verify unexpected requests — especially for money, credentials, or access — through a separate, known channel.
  • Hover over links before clicking; check the domain carefully, not just the display text.
  • Treat urgency and fear as a signal to slow down, not speed up.
  • Enable multi-factor authentication everywhere it's offered.
  • Report suspicious emails through your organization's official channel instead of just deleting them.
  • Never enable macros or "edit mode" on an unexpected attachment.
Awareness

Safety Tips

Quick reference — the short version of everything else in this dashboard.

  • No legitimate service will ever ask for your password or OTP by email or text.
  • A deadline in an email is a reason to slow down, not a reason to hurry.
  • When in doubt, contact the sender directly using a number or address you already know — never one from the message itself.
  • Bookmark your important accounts (bank, email) and use the bookmark, not a link from a message.
  • If a message feels slightly "off," trust that — attackers are counting on you not to look closely.
